# Map-aid: tailored logistics

Route decisions relied on memory and hidden availability. I designed a map-based system that surfaces nearby drivers in real time, turning flexibility into a reliable coordination resource.

## Context & Intro

### Role

Product design, service design, research

### Timeline

Two design iterations

### Focus

Product and service design for Backpack Brigade’s driver coordination layer—making informal flexibility visible, structured, and actionable when dispatch decisions happen under pressure.

### The Problem

Route decisions relied on memory and hidden availability. Spatial awareness—who is nearby and available—is the core of coordination. Everything else supports that decision.

### The Solution

I designed a map-based system that surfaces nearby drivers in real time, turning flexibility into a reliable coordination resource.

### Key Impact

Real-time visibility replaces memory-based dispatch. Coordination scales without adding cognitive load.

## Scope

- Structured driver profiles. Availability, constraints, and context captured once—so dispatch does not depend on who remembers whom.
- Map-based decision layer. Nearby, available drivers surfaced in real time so coordinators decide fast without leaving the map.
- Decision support, not automation. The system supports judgment under pressure—it does not replace the person coordinating the route.

## Before and after Backpack Brigade

Driver coordination had to work as one operational system—not texts, memory, and ad hoc calls stitched together at the warehouse door.

Field discovery with coordinators and drivers defined what “available and nearby” had to mean; map visibility, structured profiles, and dispatch entry made that signal actionable in the moment.

Service and product design connected spatial awareness, dispatch rhythm, and nonprofit scale so growth did not add coordination overhead the team could not sustain.

## What the session showed

One coordinator, unguided, through three real-week scenarios: Monday start, Wednesday routes at risk, and onboarding a new driver. Nothing broke. Not everything was obvious.

Hoyt named the job the product had to do—and the part it could not take from him.

### Product value

The only hard part is knowing who’s nearby and whether they can take the route. That’s what this solves.

### Trust / adoption

At first, I’d want to double-check everything — not because it’s wrong, but because it’s new.

### Human in the loop

The decision is still mine. The system shows me options, but I make the call.

## The map is the product

The first model assumed the system could automate most of the flow, and that Hoyt would trust it. Validation showed the opposite: he wanted to verify each action, keep direct contact—call or write—and know what was sent, and to whom.

The product is a geographic tool. Data is support. The split became eighty percent map, twenty percent data. The next validation does not measure use. It measures trust.

## Where we are now

This started as a coordination system. It is not. It is a layer over a process that already works—the overlay that makes the critical moment visible: who is available, who is nearby, what decision to take.

The interaction is refined. The next work is generating trust of use. Adoption is sometimes UX, not trust: usable, readable, and still not automatic. The coordinator needs to verify, keep control, and keep human contact.
